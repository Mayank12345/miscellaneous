Ashtavakra Gita

अष्टावक्र गीता · Aṣṭāvakra Gītā

A short, fearless conversation between a young sage and a king. Its one claim: you are already free. You only believe you are bound.

A very old story. A wise boy with a bendy body tells a king a big secret: deep inside, you are like the sky, and nothing can ever hurt the sky.

The whole teaching

In one breath

The secret, in 4 little steps

Most spiritual books give you a ladder. This one says there is no ladder, because you are already where the ladder leads. Everything else in the text unpacks these four points.

The wise boy told the king four simple things. You can learn them too.

  1. 1

    You are the awareness

    There's a watcher inside you

    You are not the body, the thoughts, or your roles. You are the knowing in which all of them show up. The text calls it sākṣī, the witness.

    Inside you is a quiet "watcher". It sees everything you see, hears everything you hear, and feels everything you feel.

  2. 2

    It is already free

    Nothing can hurt the watcher

    Awareness is never stained by what passes through it. A movie of a fire never burns the screen.

    Clouds float in the sky, but the sky never gets wet or dirty. You are like the sky.

  3. 3

    Bondage is a belief

    We feel stuck when we grab

    You feel bound when the mind grabs, pushes away, and keeps saying "I, me, mine". The chains are made of thinking (1.11, 8.1–8.4).

    When we hold things too tight ("Mine! I want it! Go away!"), we feel stuck and sad.

  4. 4

    So rest, and live

    Open your hands

    There is nothing to achieve. Recognise what you are, rest in it, and live naturally. King Janaka went on ruling a kingdom.

    Let go a little and you feel light and happy again, like a balloon. You can still play, help, and have fun.

Who was Ashtavakra?

The boy bent in eight places

The story of the bendy boy

The Gita itself tells no biography. Its teacher's life story comes from the Mahābhārata (Vana Parva, chapters 132–134), with a few scenes added by popular retellings. Each card says which.

Here is his story, in seven little pictures.

1Mahābhārata

Corrected from the womb

The baby who said "Oops!"

Sage Kahoḍa recites the Vedas near his pregnant wife Sujātā. The unborn child, already learned, points out his father's mistakes. Stung, Kahoḍa curses him to be born bent in eight places.

Before he was born, a baby heard his daddy reading and said, "Daddy, you got it wrong!" Daddy got grumpy, and the baby was born with a body bent in eight places.

2Mahābhārata

The father lost in debate

Daddy loses a contest

Kahoḍa goes to King Janaka's court to win wealth in a debate. He loses to the court scholar Vandin (Bandī), and the loser's penalty is to be drowned.

His daddy went to a big clever-question contest at the king's palace. He lost and was taken away under the water.

3Mahābhārata

Twelve years old at the gate

"No kids allowed!"

Raised by his grandfather Uddālaka beside his uncle Śvetaketu, Ashtavakra learns the truth at twelve and walks to Janaka's court. The gatekeeper refuses a child. His reply: a person is elder by knowledge, not by grey hair.

At 12 he walked to the palace. The guard said, "No kids allowed!" He said, "Being wise makes you grown-up, not being tall."

4Popular retelling

The court laughs

Everyone laughs. He laughs louder.

Scholars laugh at his twisted body. He laughs back: he came looking for wise men and found leather-dealers, people who judge only by skin. Janaka bows to him. Osho tells this version in his very first talk on the text.

Everyone laughed at his bendy body. He laughed back: "You only look at the outside!" The king felt ashamed and said sorry.

5Mahābhārata

The riddle duel

He wins!

He and Vandin trade verses counting up from one to thirteen. At thirteen Vandin falters and Ashtavakra completes the verse. Vandin reveals he is a son of Varuṇa, lord of waters; the drowned scholars were sent to his father's ritual. Kahoḍa returns.

He won the clever-question contest, and his daddy came back up from the water, safe and sound.

6Mahābhārata

Straightened in the river

A splash in a magic river

His father asks him to bathe in the Samaṅgā river. He comes out with straight limbs. (This is the moment the line at the top of the page shows.)

Daddy said sorry and asked him to splash in a special river. When he came out, his body was straight!

7Traditional frame

The king becomes the student

The king becomes the student

Janaka, a king already famous for wisdom, asks the young sage how to be truly free. The Ashtavakra Gita is that conversation.

The king asked the boy, "How can I be truly happy and free?" Their talk became this book.

Two folk legends often told with it. The dream: Janaka dreams he is a starving beggar, wakes as a king, and asks, "Which is real, this or that?" Ashtavakra answers, "Neither. You, who saw both, are real." The stirrup: Janaka asks to be taught in the time it takes to put one foot in a horse's stirrup. These are later stories, not part of the text, but they capture its spirit: freedom is a recognition, and recognition takes no time.

A bonus story! The king dreamed he was a hungry beggar. When he woke up, he asked, "Am I a king or a beggar?" The boy said, "Neither! You are the one who watched both."

See it, play with it

Five pictures the text uses

Five games that show the secret

The Ashtavakra Gita argues almost entirely through images: ocean and wave, rope and snake, space and pot. Each lab below is built from a specific verse. Play with it, then read the verse.

Each picture below is a little game. Tap the buttons and see what happens.

Picture 1

The ocean and the waves

Each wave feels separate, with its own shape, size and lifespan. Seen as water, nothing is separate and nothing is born or dies. The text says the world rises from the Self the way waves rise from water, and is never other than it. A wave does not need to become water. It only needs to notice.

Every wave thinks, "I'm me, and you're you." But they're all made of the same water! When a wave goes down, it doesn't disappear. It just becomes the ocean again.

2.4 · Waves, foam and bubbles are not different from water. So this world, flowing out of the Self, is not different from the Self.
7.1 · In me, the endless ocean, the ship of the world drifts this way and that on its own winds. I am not troubled.

Picture 2

The rope and the snake

In dim light a rope looks like a snake, and the fear is completely real. When the lamp comes on, nobody kills the snake. It simply was never there. Ashtavakra uses this to describe how the world looks when you misread who you are: separate, threatening, something to fight. Knowledge removes a misreading. It does not add anything.

In the dark, a rope can look like a scary snake! Turn on the light and... it's just a rope. You didn't have to fight anything. Lots of fears are like that.

2.7 · Not knowing the Self, the world appears; knowing the Self, it no longer appears as separate. Not knowing the rope, a snake appears; knowing it, the snake is gone.

Picture 3

The space inside the pot

The space inside a pot seems to have a shape and a location. Break the pot and that space is not freed, harmed or moved. It was always the one space. The body and mind are the pot; awareness is the space. That is why the text says there is nothing to give up and nothing to grab.

A pot has air inside it. If the pot breaks, where does the air go? Nowhere! It was always part of the big sky. You are like the sky, and your body is like the pot.

6.1 · I am endless like space; the world is like a pot. Knowing this, there is nothing to give up, nothing to grab, nothing to destroy.

The sky (awareness): untouchedThe sky (that's you!)
Picture 4

The witness: sky and clouds

Tap a cloud to let it go.

Anger, joy, worry and boredom pass through like weather. Something notices all of them and is coloured by none. Ashtavakra's opening teaching points straight at it: you are the one seer of everything, already free. Your only bondage is that you take the seer to be something other than yourself, for example a body or a mood.

Feelings are like clouds: an angry cloud, a sad cloud, a happy cloud. They float by. You are the big blue sky. Can a cloud hurt the sky? Never!

1.7 · You are the one seer of all, always free. Your bondage is only this: you see the seer as something else.
(Ashtavakra himself uses the witness and space images; the clouds are a modern teaching aid for them.)

Picture 5

Not this, not this: what is left?

Classical Indian thought builds the body and world from five elements. Remove each one in thought and see what cannot be removed.You're made of lots of things. Take them away one by one. What's left?

This is the first answer Ashtavakra gives Janaka. Whatever you can observe, such as bones, blood, warmth, breath or the space they occupy, you are the observer of it. What is left when every observable thing is set aside is not a blank. It is the knowing itself, cid-rūpa, "whose form is awareness".

You can notice your body, your breathing, being warm or cold. So who is doing the noticing? That's the real you, the watcher!

1.3 · You are not earth, water, fire, air or sky. To be free, know yourself as awareness, the witness of all these.

Chapter 8 · the shortest definition in the book

Stuck vs free: six movements of the mind

Sticky hands and open hands

Chapter 8 gives a precise checklist. You are bound whenever the mind does any of six things about anything. Freedom is not numbness. Feelings still arise; they just are not clutched. Pick a situation to see which movements fire.

There are six ways our mind gets "sticky". Pick something that might happen to you, and see which sticky feelings show up.

Bound mindSticky hands
Free mindOpen hands

The root: the word "I"

The sticky word: "ME!"

yadā nāhaṃ tadā mokṣo

All six movements need someone they belong to: "I want", "I lost", "that's mine". Verse 8.4 cuts to that root. When there is no "I", there is freedom; when there is "I", there is bondage. The advice that follows is gentle: knowing this, lightly, grab nothing and push nothing away. The word Ashtavakra uses is helayā, "playfully, without effort".

Every sticky feeling starts with "ME! MINE!" When we stop shouting "me, me, me" inside, we feel free, like a bird. You can still have toys and friends. You just don't squeeze them so tight.

8.1 · It is bondage when the mind wants something, grieves over something, rejects something, holds on to something, is thrilled by something or angry at something.
8.4 · When there is no "I", there is freedom. When there is "I", there is bondage.

How it differs from the Bhagavad Gita

Two famous "song" books

"Gītā" just means "song". The Bhagavad Gita is the famous one; the Ashtavakra Gita is shorter, stranger, and far more radical. They agree on the goal and disagree on the road.

There's another famous book called the Bhagavad Gita. Both talk about being free and happy, but in different ways.

The text and its most famous commentary

The Ashtavakra Gita vs Osho's Mahageeta

The little book and the long story-teller

In 1976–77 the teacher Osho gave 91 talks in Hindi on this text in Pune, published as the Ashtavakra Mahageeta ("the Great Gita"). For many Indian readers this is how they first meet Ashtavakra. This comparison is built from the transcripts of all 90 talks that are available, about 1.07 million words, read against the 298 verses.

There is the little book of poems. And there is a man called Osho who talked about it for a very, very long time, like a grandpa telling stories about a short poem.

The verdict

Read the Gita first. Then listen to Osho, one chapter at a time.

Read the little book first. Then listen to the stories.

The original is better for understanding: exact, complete, and finished in an evening. Osho is better for being moved. He takes one verse and spends an hour and a half making you feel it. But he speaks about the text, brings in a whole world of other teachers, and adds strong opinions the text never states. Know the source first, and you will hear exactly where Ashtavakra ends and Osho begins.

The little book tells you the secret. The stories help you feel it. Hear the secret first, so you know what the stories are about.

Who else is in the room

Who else does Osho talk about?

The Ashtavakra Gita names no one but its two speakers (and, once, the gods Shiva, Vishnu and Brahma, 16.11). Osho's talks are a crossroads of world spirituality. Buddha comes up in 88 of the 90 transcripts, more often than Janaka, who is actually in the text.

The little book only has the boy and the king. Osho talks about lots of other wise people too, like Buddha and Jesus, and tells funny stories about Mulla Nasruddin.

Text · 1.2
If you want freedom, treat the objects of the senses like poison, and drink forgiveness, honesty, kindness, contentment and truth like nectar.
Osho · talk 1
विषय का अर्थ होता है, जिसे खाने से हम बार-बार मरते हैं।
"A sense object (viṣaya) is that which, when we consume it, we die again and again."
कुटिलता विष है; सीधा-सरलपन, आर्जव अमृत है।
"Crookedness is poison; straightforward simplicity is nectar."
What he adds: he builds on the sound-link between viṣaya (sense object) and viṣa (poison), and moves the poison inside. It is not the world that kills but the endless craving for more of it. That steers the verse away from "run from the world", which suits both the text and Janaka, who never left his throne.He says the poison isn't toys or sweets. It's always wanting MORE. Being simple and kind is the yummy medicine.
Text · 1.7
You are the one seer of all, always free. Your bondage is only this: you see the seer as something else.
Osho · talk 3 (English edition)
"Your bondage is this: you see the other, not yourself, as the observer."
What he adds: a social twist. Osho extends "the other as observer" to living through other people's eyes and opinions, so the verse becomes a diagnosis of approval-seeking. It is illuminating, and it is his extension.He says: we feel stuck when we worry what everyone else thinks of us.
Text · 1.15
You are unattached, actionless, self-shining and spotless. Your bondage is just this: you keep practising samādhi.
Osho · talk 5
पतंजलि कहते हैं, चित्त-वृत्तियों को शांत करो तो तुम आत्मा को जान लोगे। अष्टावक्र कहते हैं, आत्मा को जान लो, चित्त-वृत्तियां शांत हो जायेंगी।
"Patanjali says: quiet the movements of the mind and you will know the Self. Ashtavakra says: know the Self, and the movements of the mind will quiet down."
Where they pull apart: this is Osho at his clearest. Yoga works from the mind toward the Self; Ashtavakra works from the Self outward, like lighting a lamp instead of fighting the darkness with swords (his image). Yet the question-and-answer talk the day before is titled "Meditation: The Only Medicine", and Osho was famous for meditation techniques. His bridge is that medicine is for an illness and is put away once you are well. The text itself offers no such bridge; it simply says practice can bind.Other teachers say, "Make your mind quiet, then you'll find yourself." Ashtavakra says, "Find yourself, and your mind gets quiet by itself." Like switching on a light instead of chasing the dark away.
Text · 6.1–6.4
I am endless like space; the world is like a pot. Knowing this, there is nothing to give up, nothing to grab, nothing to destroy.
Osho · talk 21
एक लंगोटी काफी है; कोई साम्राज्य नहीं चाहिए पकड़ने को। ... त्याग भी आदमी पकड़ ले सकता है।
"A loincloth is enough; you don't need an empire to cling to. ... A person can cling even to renunciation."
What he adds: a sharp warning against spiritual showing-off, told through a Sufi story of a proud friend who became a famous naked ascetic. It fits the verse's "nothing to give up" closely. This theme, renunciation as another form of grasping, runs through the series: the word for renunciation (tyāg) appears in 64 of the 90 talks.He says: giving everything away to show off is still grabbing. Grabbing "I'm so good!" instead of toys.
Text · 8.4
When there is no "I", there is freedom. When there is "I", there is bondage. Knowing this, grab nothing and push nothing away.
Osho · talk 25
सरल, अनूठे, पर अति कठिन! जितने सरल उतने कठिन। ... कठिन को तो करने में अहंकार को रस आता है।
"Simple, unique, yet extremely hard! As hard as they are simple. ... The ego takes delight in doing what is difficult."
What he adds: a reason why the shortest teaching is the hardest to take: the ego wants a challenge it can win. He illustrates it with Diogenes asking Alexander what he will do after conquering the world ("Then we'll rest"). The insight is his, and it explains why chapter 8 is only four verses long.He says: we like hard things because we can show off. But this one is easy, and that's why it's tricky!
Text · 16.11
Even if Shiva, Vishnu or Brahma were your teacher, you would not rest in yourself until you forgot everything.
Osho · talk 47
जब तक तू सब न भूल जाये जो बाहर से सीखा ... न रहो हिंदू न मुसलमान न ईसाई न जैन न बौद्ध।
"Until you forget all that you learned from outside ... be neither Hindu nor Muslim nor Christian nor Jain nor Buddhist."
Where he goes beyond it: the Sanskrit says simply "forgetting everything" (sarva-vismaraṇa). Osho narrows it to what you learned "from outside", then widens it into dropping every religious label. That is a bold, characteristic Osho move, and a defensible one, but the verse itself names no religions.The little book says: forget everything and just be. Osho adds: and don't worry about which religion you belong to.
Text · everywhere
Never mentions Krishna or the Bhagavad Gita, and never compares itself with any other teaching.
Osho · talk 1
कृष्ण की गीता खिचड़ी जैसी है ... कुटिल राजनीतिज्ञ थे, डिप्लोमैट थे।
"Krishna's Gita is like khichdi [a mixed porridge] ... he was a cunning politician, a diplomat."
Where he goes beyond it: this is Osho's polemic, not Ashtavakra's. He argues the Bhagavad Gita became popular because everyone can find their own path in it, while Ashtavakra makes no compromise. Stirring, and sometimes unfair. Krishna still comes up in 72 talks, so the comparison is a running thread, not a one-off.Osho says this book is better than another famous book. That's his opinion! The little book never says that about itself.
Text · chapter 20
Janaka's last verses ask "where?" again and again (where is bondage, where is freedom, where is teacher or student?) and the book ends in silence.
Osho · talk 91, the last
बस इतना ही सारसूत्र है अष्टावक्र और जनक के इस परम संवाद का, इतना ही--स्मृति लौट आयी।
"Just this is the essence of this supreme dialogue between Ashtavakra and Janaka, just this: memory returned."
What he adds: a one-line summary of the whole book: nothing was gained, something was remembered. There is an irony here. "Memory regained" (smṛtir labdhā) is what Arjuna says at the very end of the Bhagavad Gita (18.73), the book Osho spent his first talk criticising.At the very end, Osho says the whole story is about one thing: the king remembered who he really was.

What the question-and-answer talks are

The question days

Half the series answers questions from Osho's listeners, who address him as "Bhagwan". Many questions are about their own meditation experiences ("during yesterday's talk I felt I was not on this earth") or about the practices of his movement, such as whether ochre robes and the mala help (talk 4). These talks are pastoral guidance for a community as much as commentary. If you want Ashtavakra, the odd-numbered talks are the core; the even ones are optional.

On question days, people told Osho how they felt, and he answered them. Those are extra. The poem days are the main ones.

Read it carefully

Four common misreadings

Things it does NOT mean

A text this radical is easy to bend into something convenient. The verses themselves block these readings.

Some people get the secret a bit wrong. Here's what it does NOT say.

"Nothing matters, so I can do anything."

"Then I can be mean!"

The very first instruction (1.2) asks for forgiveness, honesty, kindness, contentment and truth, "like nectar". Chapter 3 scolds Janaka for any leftover greed. Seeing through the ego makes cruelty pointless, because cruelty is the ego's work.

No! The wise boy says the first thing to do is be kind, honest and forgiving. People who feel free are usually the kindest.

"It says the world is fake."

"So my family isn't real?"

It says the world is not separate from awareness, as waves are not separate from water (2.4). The snake vanishes; the rope remains. What dissolves is the sense of a threatening "other".

Your family is real and you love them! The book means everyone is made of the same "water", like waves in one ocean.

"Meditation is useless."

"So sitting quietly is silly?"

Verse 1.15 warns against practising in order to become something, because that keeps the "I who is not there yet" alive. Quiet sitting is fine; treating it as a ladder to a freedom you lack is the trap.

Sitting quietly is lovely! Just don't worry about doing it "perfectly". You are already okay.

"It's only for monks in caves."

"Only grown-ups can understand it."

The student is a working king with a court, family and wars to manage. Chapter 18 describes the free person acting fully in the world, "like a child", without the burden of "I am doing this".

The teacher was a kid, and he taught a king! You can get it too.

Try the witness for sixty seconds

The Watcher Game

This is not a technique to gain anything. It is a way to check verse 1.4 for yourself: "if you just rest in awareness, seeing yourself as distinct from the body, right now you will be happy and at peace."

Play this with a grown-up. Sit still, press start, and follow the words.

For you to tell · about 10 minutes

Explaining it to a 5-year-old

A ready-made script. Use real objects; young children understand with their hands first. Say the bold lines, do the actions, then ask the question and wait for their answer. There are no wrong answers.

A bowl of waterA piece of stringA torch or phone lightA paper cupA banana (yes, really)
Listen to the story · about 2½ minutes
A warm, deep storyteller's voice reads each step and pauses so your child can answer.

  1. "Once there was a boy whose body was bent in eight places. People laughed at him. But he was the wisest person in the whole kingdom."

    Do: Hold up a curvy banana.

    Ask: "Is a bendy banana less yummy than a straight one?" (The inside matters, not the outside.)

  2. "A king asked the boy, 'How can I be happy all the time, and never feel stuck?' The boy said, 'You already are! Let me show you.'"

    Ask: "When do you feel stuck or grumpy?"

  3. "Look! Each wave thinks it's all alone. But every wave is made of water. When a wave goes down, it doesn't disappear. It goes back into the big water."

    Do: Make little waves in the bowl with your finger.

    Ask: "Is this wave water? Is that one? Are you and me made of the same 'water' too?"

  4. "Feelings are like clouds. There's an angry cloud, a sad cloud, a happy cloud. They float by. But you are the big sky. Clouds can't hurt the sky."

    Do: Point at the sky, or draw a sky with clouds.

    Ask: "What colour cloud do you feel today? Is the sky still there behind it?"

  5. "In the dark, things can look scary. But when we turn on the light..."

    Do: Dim the lights, wiggle the string on the floor, "Is it a snake?!", then switch on the torch.

    Ask: "Was there ever a snake? Did we have to fight it?"

  6. "This cup has air inside. If I squish the cup, where does the air go?"

    Do: Squash the paper cup gently.

    Ask: "Did the air get hurt? Your body is like the cup. The real you is like the air and the sky."

  7. "Let's play the Watcher Game. Close your eyes. Listen. Can you hear a sound? Who's hearing it? There's a quiet you inside that notices everything. That watcher is always okay."

    Ask: "Can the watcher get scared? Can it get old?"

  8. "Goodnight. It's okay to feel sad or cross sometimes. Feelings are clouds. You are the sky. You are already okay, just as you are."

A tip: never use the sky idea to brush off a child's real feelings. Name the feeling first ("You're really sad"), comfort them, and only later, when they're calm, play the cloud game.

History, influence, and how to read it

Where did this book come from?

Also called the Aṣṭāvakra Saṃhitā, it belongs to Advaita Vedānta, the Indian school that holds reality is "not-two": the Self (ātman) and the absolute (brahman) are one. Its author and date are unknown. Proposed dates range from soon after the Bhagavad Gita to the 14th century CE; many scholars place it in the medieval period, in the milieu of Śaṅkara's Advaita (8th century onward).

Its characters are older than the text. Janaka appears in the Upaniṣads as the king who questions the sage Yājñavalkya, and Ashtavakra's life story is in the Mahābhārata. The Gita borrows these figures to stage a teaching with no rituals, no gods to please and no stages to climb.

Its modern fame owes much to Ramakrishna, who had the young Narendranath (later Swami Vivekananda) read it aloud; the young man at first found its claims shocking. Ramana Maharshi cited it, Osho gave a long lecture series on it (Mahāgītā), and English readers know it through Thomas Byrom's The Heart of Awareness, Bart Marshall's version, John Richards's free translation and Swami Nityaswarupananda's Sanskrit-English edition.

Nobody knows who wrote it or exactly when. It's very old, hundreds and hundreds of years. Many famous teachers in India loved it and read it to their students.
